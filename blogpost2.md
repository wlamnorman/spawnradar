# How to Write Creator Outreach Messages That Actually Get Read

*Published by SpawnRadar · 6 min read*

---

Most outreach emails for indie games get deleted in under five seconds.

Not because creators are dismissive. Because most emails look identical: a long paragraph about how much work went into the game, a vague description of the genre, and a request for coverage that offers nothing specific to that creator.

Creators who cover games regularly have seen this email hundreds of times. They have learned to move past it fast.

This guide breaks down the structure of a message that actually gets read, with templates you can adapt for YouTube, streaming, and community outreach.

---

## Why Most Pitches Fail

Before the templates, the diagnosis.

**They are not personalized.** Saying "I love your content" without referencing anything specific signals immediately that you sent the same message to fifty people. Creators know this. It makes your game feel like a quantity play, not a quality fit.

**They lead with the developer, not the creator.** The first paragraph is often about how long the game took to make, how passionate the team is, or how unique the concept is. The creator does not know you. They need a reason to care about your game before they care about you.

**They are too long.** Every extra sentence reduces the chance the email gets read. A creator opening email on their phone between videos is not going to read four paragraphs. They are going to read the first two sentences and decide.

**The ask is unclear.** Ending with "let me know what you think!" is not an ask. Creators want to know exactly what you are offering and what you are requesting, so they can make a quick yes or no decision.

---

## The Structure That Works

A high-performing outreach message has five components, in this order:

1. **Their name** — correct and specific
2. **Why you chose them specifically** — one sentence, referencing their actual content
3. **What the game is** — genre, premise, one hook sentence
4. **The offer** — what you are giving them (key, press access, exclusive window)
5. **The links** — trailer and press kit, nothing else

The whole thing should be readable in 20–30 seconds. If it is not, cut it until it is.

## Template 1: YouTube Channel Outreach

This is the most common and highest-value contact type. Use it when you have identified a channel that covers your genre and has posted recently.

---

**Subject:** [Game Name] — [Genre] game, key available for [Channel Name]

Hi [Creator Name],

Watched your recent video on [specific game they covered] — your breakdown of [something specific about that video] was exactly the kind of coverage I was hoping to find.

I'm reaching out about [Game Name], a [genre] game about [one-sentence premise]. It's [one clear differentiator — what makes it unusual or interesting to cover]. Releases [date / "this month"].

I'd love to offer you a key. No coverage obligation — if it's not a fit for your channel, no worries at all.

Trailer: [link]
Press kit: [link]

Happy to answer any questions.

[Your name]
[Studio / solo dev name]
[Email]

---

**Notes on this template:**

The subject line includes the channel name. This sounds small but it signals immediately that the email is not a blast. It also makes it easy for the creator to find it later when they search their inbox.

The reference to a specific video is the most important sentence in the email. It needs to be real. Creators can tell when someone watched something versus when someone read the title. Pick something from their last three videos and reference something specific — a comment they made, a game mechanic they highlighted, a moment in the video.

"No coverage obligation" lowers friction significantly. It reframes the email from a transaction to a genuine offer. Creators who feel pressured into coverage either ignore the email or produce unenthusiastic videos.

## Template 2: Twitch Streamer Outreach

Streamers think differently than YouTubers. YouTube is archive-first — a video performs over months. Streaming is live-first — the value is in the moment and in clips. Adjust accordingly.

---

**Subject:** [Game Name] — live-friendly [genre], key for [Channel Name]

Hey [Creator Name],

I've been watching your streams of [specific genre or game they play] — the way your chat reacts to [specific moment type — e.g. "surprise mechanics" or "punishing deaths"] made me think [Game Name] could be a strong fit.

[Game Name] is a [genre] game that [one-sentence premise]. It's designed to [one thing that makes it good for streaming — e.g. "generate strong chat moments", "has short sessions that work well between breaks", "has a clear tension arc per run"].

Key available now, no strings attached. Happy to be available during a stream if you have questions.

Trailer: [link]
Press kit: [link]

[Your name]
[Email]

---

**Notes on this template:**

The "designed to [streaming quality]" line matters a lot for streamers. They are always thinking about how a game will feel for their audience in real time, not just whether the game is good. Tell them explicitly why it will work live.

Offering to be available during the stream is a nice touch for smaller streamers who might have questions about mechanics or lore. Do not say this unless you actually mean it and have the time.

## Template 3: Community / Forum Post

When you are posting in a subreddit, Discord, or forum where your target players already gather, the format is different. You are not pitching — you are sharing. The post should feel like a member of the community sharing something relevant, not an ad.

---

**Title:** I made a [genre] game for people who like [specific reference point] — would love feedback from this community

Hey everyone,

I've been lurking in [community name] for a while and wanted to share something I've been working on.

[Game Name] is a [genre] game that [one-sentence premise]. I built it because [genuine reason relevant to this community — e.g. "I couldn't find a puzzle game that treated the player as an adult" or "I wanted a strategy game with the depth of X but shorter sessions"].

It's [release status — demo available / launching soon / just launched]. [One thing that's specifically relevant to this community's interests.]

Demo / store page: [link]
Trailer: [link]

Happy to answer questions about the design, the development, anything. Genuinely interested in feedback from people in this community.

---

**Notes on this template:**

Community posts live or die by authenticity. If you have genuinely been part of the community, say so. If you haven't, do not pretend. Just be direct: "I found this community while researching where [genre] fans gather."

The "I built it because" line is the most important. It gives the community a reason to care that is rooted in shared taste, not marketing. Communities are very good at detecting when a developer actually gets what they love about the genre versus when they are targeting them as a demographic.

Do not post a community pitch and then disappear. Check back for comments. Reply to questions. This is not just courtesy — it is what turns a post from a one-time spike into ongoing community presence.

---

## Personalisation: What Actually Needs to Change

Many developers read template advice and conclude they need to rewrite every email from scratch. You do not. Most of the body can stay the same across contacts. What must change:

- **The creator's name** — obviously, but double-check the spelling
- **The specific video or content reference** — this is non-negotiable, do not skip it
- **The subject line** — include their channel name or handle
- **The streaming-specific hook** — if you are contacting a streamer vs. a YouTuber

Everything else — the game description, the offer, the links — can be templated. The personalisation is in the hook, not the pitch.

---

## After You Send

Keep a simple log. A spreadsheet is fine:

| Creator | Platform | Sent | Response | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [name] | YouTube | [date] | Yes | Key sent |
| [name] | Twitch | [date] | No reply | Follow up [date] |

One follow-up after 10–14 days is fine. Keep it short:

> "Hey [name], just following up on my message from [date] about [Game Name]. Happy to send a key if you're interested — no pressure either way."

That is all. Do not send a third message. Move on.
