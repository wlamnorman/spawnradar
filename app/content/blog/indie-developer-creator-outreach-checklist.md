You finished your game. Now you need people to actually see it.

For indie developers, content creators are one of the few channels that can move the needle fast. A single well-matched video can generate more attention than weeks of posting into the void. But most outreach fails long before a creator ever replies. The problem usually is not the game. It is the preparation, the targeting or the message.

This is the checklist we recommend using before reaching out to a single creator.

## Before You Contact Anyone

These are not optional. If you skip them, you are wasting your time and burning goodwill. Think "frictionless" for the content creator. The goal is simple: make it as easy as possible for a creator to say yes.

### ✅ Your press kit is ready

Creators need assets. If you do not give them good raw material, they either skip your game or make something that undersells it.

Your press kit should include:

- **Key art**: high resolution, ideally with the main character or focal subject isolated on a transparent background
- **Logo**: clean, readable, with and without a background
- **Gameplay screenshots**: real gameplay, not cutscenes, at least 1920×1080
- **A trailer**: gameplay-first, under 90 seconds, with the strongest footage in the opening seconds
- **A short factsheet**: release date, platform, genre, one-sentence description and contact details

If you are using Google Drive, make sure the folder is accessible to anyone with the link. Most creators will not ask for access.


### ✅ The creator can play your game immediately


Do not do outreach unless you are ready to hand over access the moment someone says yes. Delay kills momentum.

What that looks like depends on the platform:

- **Steam**: have keys generated and ready to send
- **Browser**: make sure the game loads directly without accounts, invites or friction
- **Mobile**: have a TestFlight link or Play Store invite ready
- **Pre-release build**: an itch.io page with password access is often the simplest option

The goal is zero friction between "yes" and playing. Every extra step is another chance for the creator to move on.

### ✅ Your music is cleared for content creation

This is an easy mistake to miss and an expensive one to make.

If your game includes licensed music that triggers Content ID, a creator’s video can be demonetized, muted or restricted the moment it goes live. On Twitch, the VOD may be muted. Creators who have been burned by this before will often avoid your game entirely.

If there is any doubt, either secure rights that clearly cover third-party streaming and video use or replace the track.

| Tier | Subscriber Range | Notes |
|------|-----------------|-------|
| Micro | 1K – 20K | High response rate, genuine enthusiasm, loyal communities |
| Mid | 20K – 150K | Best balance of reach and response rate |
| Large | 150K – 500K | Much lower response rate, but worth trying if the fit is perfect |

Do not dismiss micro creators. They are often the most thorough, the most responsive and the most likely to build a long-term relationship with a game they love. Also note that a 500K channel that mostly covers AAA action games is usually less valuable than a 15K channel that consistently covers games like yours. Fit matters more than raw reach.

## The message itself

Your outreach message does not need to be clever. It needs to be clear, specific and easy to act on.

Creators can tell immediately when they are being sent a generic pitch. A good message shows that you chose them for a reason, explains the game quickly and makes the next step obvious.

Use this checklist before sending:

- I addressed them by the name they actually use
- I explained why I chose them specifically
- I described the game in one or two clear sentences
- I included a trailer link
- I included the press kit
- I made the ask clear
- I kept the body under 200 words

## The Mistakes Worth Highlighting

**Reaching out publicly.** Posting something like “hey @CreatorName check out my game!” in public is almost never a good idea unless you already have a relationship.

**Contacting creators at the wrong time.** Avoid pitching during major Steam sales, Nintendo Directs, major releases in your genre and obvious holiday or summer slow periods.

**Sending the same email to twenty people at once.** Generic outreach gets noticed for the wrong reasons. If your message could be sent to anyone, it probably should not be sent at all.

**Treating a non-response as a rejection.** Creators get a constant stream of pitches. No reply usually means the message was missed, not that the answer was no. One polite follow-up after 10 to 14 days is reasonable.
