A YouTuber with 4 million subscribers shared exactly what makes a good pitch email versus a bad one. His priority hierarchy is worth memorising:

1. Making entertaining content for their audience
2. Paying their bills
3. Promoting your game

Your game is third on their list. That's not cynicism — it's how the economics work. Understanding this changes how you write every pitch.

---

## The email that works

The best pitch email a creator cited as an example was from Raw Fury for Dome Keeper. It was described in one line: no bloat, quick and simple, easy to understand.

Here's what that means in practice:

**Include the essentials in the first email:**
- What the game is (one sentence)
- A trailer link
- A Steam page link
- 2-3 screenshots or key art
- Whether it's early access or full launch
- Whether there's an embargo date

**Don't make them work for it.** If you say "Steam key inside," include the actual key. If you reference your game, assume they know nothing about it — they get hundreds of these emails.

## The emails that get deleted

**The generic blast.** Creators can tell when they're one of 400 recipients. If your email doesn't reference anything specific about their channel, it reads like spam.

**The DM pitch.** Use email, not Twitter DMs, Instagram messages, or Discord. Every creator who's spoken publicly about this says the same thing: email is for business, DMs are for fans.

**The repeated follow-up.** One follow-up months later with a genuine update (new trailer, launch date confirmed) is fine. Three follow-ups in two weeks is not. You're not the only developer in their inbox.

**The comparison trap.** Saying "our game is like Hades meets Celeste" makes them want to play Hades and Celeste. Instead, describe what makes your game unique — the specific mechanic, the twist, the thing that would make their audience say "I need to see more of this."

## Finding creators worth pitching

The biggest waste of time in creator outreach isn't writing bad emails — it's sending good emails to the wrong people.

A perfectly crafted pitch to a creator who doesn't cover your genre is still a wasted email. They might take the key out of politeness. They won't make a video.

The developers who report the best response rates consistently say the same thing: they targeted creators who already cover games in their genre. Not the biggest channels. Not the ones with the most subscribers. The ones who have a track record of playing games like theirs.

One developer on Reddit described emailing about 30 creators immediately after their demo launch — all carefully chosen based on genre fit. That targeted approach consistently outperforms the 400-email blast in both response rate and actual coverage.

## The timeline

- **During development:** Follow creators in your genre. Watch their content. Engage genuinely on social media. This isn't a strategy — it's how you learn who actually fits.
- **Demo launch:** Email your first wave. Include a key. These are your warmest leads.
- **Pre-launch (1-2 months out):** Broader outreach. Include launch date, embargo if any, and all materials.
- **Launch week:** Final wave to anyone you missed, plus follow-up to creators who showed interest but haven't covered it yet.

## What you're really optimising for

The goal isn't to maximise the number of emails sent. It's to maximise the number of creators who actually play your game on camera.

That means:
- Better targeting (creators who fit your genre)
- Better materials (trailer, screenshots, key — all in the first email)
- Better timing (early enough for them to schedule, not so early that they forget)
- Better personalisation (one sentence showing you know their channel)

The developers who crack this aren't the ones with the biggest marketing budgets. They're the ones who put in the upfront work of finding the right 30 creators instead of blasting the wrong 400.
