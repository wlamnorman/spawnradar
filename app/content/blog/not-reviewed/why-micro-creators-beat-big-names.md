You have a game, a launch date, and a limited marketing budget. Your instinct says go big — land a creator with millions of followers, get one viral video, and watch the wishlists roll in.

Here's why that almost never works for indie games, and what to do instead.

---

## The engagement gap is real

A creator with 200K subscribers who covers roguelikes gets roughly 25% engagement on their videos. A mega-creator with 10M subscribers who covers everything gets about 5%. That's not a marginal difference — it's a 5x multiplier on the people who actually watch, click, and wishlist.

For an indie game, those engaged viewers are worth far more than passive ones. Someone watching a dedicated strategy-game channel is already in the market for your tactical roguelite. Someone watching a variety entertainer might enjoy the clip and scroll on.

## The math of many small sparks

Wishlists don't come from one big explosion. They accumulate from dozens of small moments: a 5K-subscriber YouTuber does a first impressions video, a Twitch streamer with 200 concurrent viewers plays it on a Tuesday afternoon, a clip gets shared in a Discord server.

None of these individually move the needle. But over weeks, they compound. By launch day, you've built a pool of interested players who get that launch notification email — and that's when the momentum hits.

One developer on r/gamedev reported that emailing 400 creators produced single-digit response rates. The creators who did respond and play the game? Mostly mid-tier channels with genuine genre interest, not the big names at the top of the list.

## How to find the right micro-creators

The manual approach is brutal: search YouTube for your genre, watch hours of content to assess fit, dig through channel descriptions for email addresses, build a spreadsheet, send personalised pitches. For 400 creators, that's weeks of work.

The shortcut is to think in terms of game neighbourhoods. Your game sits in a cluster of similar titles — games that share genres, themes, and mechanics. The creators who already stream or make videos about those games are your highest-value targets. They've already proven interest in your neighbourhood.

If your game is a tactical roguelite, you want creators who play FTL, Into the Breach, and Slay the Spire — not creators who played one strategy game three years ago. The more games in your neighbourhood they cover, the better the fit.

## What to look for beyond subscriber count

When evaluating a creator, check:

- **Genre consistency.** Do they regularly cover games like yours, or was it a one-off?
- **Recent activity.** A channel with 50K subs but no uploads in 3 months won't help you.
- **Engagement signals.** Comments, clip views, and VOD watch time matter more than follower count.
- **Contact availability.** If you can't find an email, they probably don't want cold pitches.

## Start earlier than you think

Creators need lead time. At minimum, one week before launch to produce a video. Ideally, you're reaching out during your demo phase — you get early feedback, creators get early access to content, and you build a relationship that pays off at launch.

Every week you wait is a week of compounding creator coverage you're missing.

## The bottom line

You don't need one big creator. You need 20-50 small ones who actually fit your game's neighbourhood. Their audiences overlap with your target players, their engagement rates are higher, and they're far more likely to respond to a genuine, personalised pitch from an indie developer.

The challenge isn't knowing this — it's doing the work of finding those 50 creators efficiently.
