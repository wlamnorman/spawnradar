# How to Time Your Creator Outreach for Maximum Launch Impact

*Published by SpawnRadar · 5 min read*

---

The most common mistake in creator outreach is not the message — it is the timing.

Developers reach out too late, scramble to send keys the week of launch, and then wonder why coverage trickles in after the algorithm has already moved on. Or they reach out too early, creators play the game and lose interest before the launch window arrives, and the coverage lands at the wrong moment for wishlist conversion.

Getting the timing right is not complicated, but it requires thinking through the process in reverse — starting with your launch date and working backwards.

---

## Why Timing Matters So Much

YouTube gaming coverage has a short half-life for discovery. A video published the week of launch, when your game is appearing in Steam's New Releases section, will generate far more traffic than the same video published three weeks later when your game has left the new releases algorithm. The algorithm and the coverage need to align.

Creators also have their own production schedules. A YouTuber who posts twice a week has videos pre-planned days or weeks in advance. If you send a key the day before launch with a hint that you hope for coverage, you are asking them to drop their existing plan for you. Most won't. The ones who will often produce rushed content.

---

## The Timeline That Works

### 6–8 weeks before launch: awareness outreach

At this stage you are not asking for coverage. You are planting seeds with creators you have identified as strong fits.

This looks like: a short email introducing your game, a trailer link, a note that you are planning to send keys to a limited number of creators 3–4 weeks out. You are not asking them to commit. You are letting them know you exist.

Why this works: creators see hundreds of keys arrive unannounced. A game they heard about in advance, from a developer who seemed thoughtful, is more likely to actually get played.

What to avoid: over-explaining your game, making it sound like a formal pitch, or asking for any commitment this early.

### 3–4 weeks before launch: key distribution

This is the primary outreach window. You are offering keys and making the timing explicit.

The email is short: you are launching on [date], you thought this creator was a strong fit based on [specific reason], here is a key with no coverage obligation, here is the press kit.

Three to four weeks gives creators time to work your game into their schedule without it feeling rushed. For high-replay-value games — roguelikes, strategy, sandbox — this is the minimum time you want to give. For short narrative games or games with a strong first-play arc, you want keys in hands 1–3 weeks before launch so the first-play experience is fresh.

### 1 week before launch: follow-up window

If you sent keys 3–4 weeks ago and haven't heard back, a single brief follow-up is appropriate here. Keep it to two sentences:

> "Hey [name], just wanted to check whether [Game Name] landed in your inbox — happy to resend the key if needed. Launch is [date]. No pressure either way."

Do not ask whether they plan to cover the game. Do not suggest a specific publish date. Just check in.

### Launch week: day-one key offers

Some creators only cover games that are already out — they don't want to cover early access or pre-release builds. Keep a short list of creators for day-one outreach. The message changes: you are not giving them lead time for planning, you are giving them an opportunity to be among the first.

This works best for creators who produce fast-turnaround content and have an audience that treats their coverage as a recommendation, not a preview.

## Platform-Specific Timing Adjustments

### YouTube

Standard 3–4 week window. A creator needs to play the game, record, edit, and schedule. The best-case scenario is a video that goes live 0–3 days after your launch date.

### Twitch

Streamers work on shorter notice. One to two weeks is usually sufficient. Streaming doesn't require editing time — a streamer who decides to play your game on Tuesday can stream it Wednesday. But they still need time to work it into their schedule and avoid conflicts with other games they planned to stream.

### Short-form (YouTube Shorts, TikTok)

Fast turnaround. If you have relationships with creators in this space, reaching out 1–2 weeks before launch is fine. The content is short and the production cycle is compressed.

## What to Avoid

**Reaching out during major releases in your genre.** If a major game in a similar genre launches the same week as your game, creators will be focused on that game. Their audience wants content about the big release. Check the Steam release calendar and any major announcements before setting your launch date.

**Launching during Steam sales.** Steam runs major sales events (Summer Sale, Winter Sale, Publisher Sales) that dominate player attention. Launches close to sales events tend to get less visibility both in creator content and in organic discovery.

**Contacting during the holidays.** Many creators take time off in late December and in summer. A key sent during a break sits unopened.

**Setting tight embargo windows.** Unless your game has a major story that needs to be kept secret, avoid embargo agreements. They create logistical overhead and give creators one more reason not to cover you. A note saying "please don't publish before [date]" is usually sufficient and easier to work with.

## Building a Launch Calendar

A simple spreadsheet with three columns is enough:

| Creator | Key sent | Follow-up date |
|---|---|---|
| [name] | [date] | [date +14 days] |
| [name] | [date] | [date +14 days] |

Track who you contacted, when, whether they responded, and when your follow-up window opens. This takes thirty minutes to set up and prevents the most common failure mode: contacting someone twice because you forgot you already reached out.

---

## A Note on Managing Expectations

No outreach timing will guarantee coverage. Creators receive many more pitches than they can play, and the decision to cover a game is usually about the game itself — whether it is interesting to play and interesting to watch — not about the logistics of the pitch.

What timing does is remove friction. A well-timed pitch from a developer who seems organised is easier to say yes to than a scrambled last-minute request from someone who seems overwhelmed. You cannot force coverage, but you can make it easy.
